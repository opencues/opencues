# Cross domain pollination & _ shaped people

"Back in the day" terms like a Jack of All trades were seen both positively and negatively as things to aspire to.

In the era of AI I am finding myself being a Jack of All trades (13 years years deep) to be sowing unexpected fruit in regards to exploring and determining the utility of LLM systems. Prior to the LLM era especially the agentic era most individuals were so busy working within their domain that they could never feasibly take the time to master another and maintain sufficient quality of execution, only the 'chosen ones' could context switch whilst keeping their skills sharp.

Thanks to agentic tools Jacks of All Trades have been supercharged with an ability to amplify every trade they explored and with the confidence to explore unknown paths being second nature to them they are likely to be the individuals who will explore the cross-sections of what LLMs can offer across different industries.

During my time moderating the r/ClaudeAI subreddit I have observed individuals are very reluctant to get creative outside of their own domain of expertise - yet it has never been easier to upskill in an unknown domain.

Another key limitation I observe when individuals are prompting LLM systems is despite the LLM being skilled it does not exactly help the user with discovering the bounds of a domain. So just because you request an LLM to make it a pretty website, does not mean it will utilise HTML Canvas or WGSL shaders, SVG Filters or Lottie animations. These concepts are not necessarily within the scope of the default website made by an LLM and thus require a practitioner to 'know' of the terms to be able to steer the LLM towards better outputs. This kind of 'knowledge-gap' exists across many domains and is a reason I encourage individuals to study different domains. I know how to code 'enough to be dangerous' is the old saying, now it is that you must know multiple domains enough to know what to prompt.

### B. The taxonomy of shape-people

Worth defining once, in one place, so the rest of the post stands on its own:

- **I-shaped** — deep specialist, narrow. One column of expertise.
- **T-shaped** — one deep specialty + broad knowledge across other areas. The cross-bar is the breadth, the vertical is the depth.
- **π-shaped** (or M-shaped) — *multiple* deep specialties + broad knowledge. Two or more verticals plus a cross-bar.
- **`_`-shaped** — all breadth, no single deep specialty. Historically considered weaker than I-shaped or T-shaped because depth produced output and breadth produced talk.

The argument the post is making: in the LLM era, the depth column is partially substitutable. A harness can encode a specialist's moves so the user does not have to carry them in working memory. Which means the *_*-shape — previously the weakest — has just become structurally stronger, because every harness the generalist plugs into adds an effective specialty without requiring years of practice to maintain.

The shape that used to be a weakness is now a substrate.

### D. "Knowing what to prompt" as the core 21st-century skill

The strongest single line in the post deserves elevation, ideally as a blockquote near the top:

> Knowing what to prompt is the core skill of the LLM era.

Historical analogue: knowing *what to look up in the library*. The library was always there. The skill that mattered was knowing the right keywords to type into the catalogue and the right shelves to walk past. LLMs are very fast librarians. The user still has to know the keywords.

Domain breadth = vocabulary breadth = better prompting. A user who knows the words `WGSL`, `SVG filter`, `Lottie`, `HTML Canvas` will produce a different website from a user who only knows `pretty website`. The model is the same. The substrate of knowledge the user carries to the prompt is different. The user with more vocabulary gets more out of the same model.

This reframes the old "Jack of all trades, master of none" critique: the modern continuation is *but oftentimes better than master of one*, originally argued by polymath defenders for centuries, and now mechanically true because LLMs let breadth compound where it previously decayed.

### E. The "harness preserves your skill" angle expanded

Wilfred touches this lightly. The expansion worth making:

A harness is the *productisation of expert moves in a domain*. The expert's lived memory of "in this situation, do this" gets encoded into prompts, cue libraries, blank scripts, transform rules. Once encoded, the harness carries those moves on the expert's behalf. The expert no longer has to keep them in active working memory.

Connect this back to OpenCues directly: cue-blank packs (legal, medical, financial) are exactly this. They encode domain vocabulary the user does not have to carry around. A user who has *never* practised law can still produce well-formed legal text by typing through the legal cue pack — the pack is the harness, and the harness is doing the specialist's job.

The implication for `_`-shaped people: previously, breadth came at the cost of depth, because depth required active practice. With harnesses, depth can be borrowed. A breadth-focused person can plug into a legal harness one week, a medical harness the next, and produce competent output in both domains during those weeks. Their breadth is now *operationally* multi-deep, even though their internal mental model is still flat.

This is the structural shift the post is reaching for: harnesses convert breadth into depth-on-demand. The Jack of all trades plus the right harnesses *is* the master of many.

### G. Why r/ClaudeAI users stay in-domain — the cause

Wilfred observes the reluctance. The cause worth naming:

People default to what they already know works. Cross-domain experimentation requires *generating hypotheses about what to try* — guessing that "maybe this technique from another field would help here" — which is genuinely harder than executing on existing patterns inside a known field.

LLMs lower the cost of *execution* dramatically. They do not lower the cost of *hypothesis generation*. The user still has to imagine the bridge before the LLM can build it.

Generalists have a hypothesis-generation advantage *because* they have more raw material to recombine. Specialists imagine bridges within their field; generalists imagine bridges between fields. Both are valuable. But in an era where execution is cheap, hypothesis generation is the binding constraint, and the people with the most cross-pollinable raw material win the marginal game.

This is also why the post's call-to-action — "study other domains" — is structurally correct: it is investment in hypothesis-generation capacity, which is exactly the cognitive resource the era is rewarding.

### H. The "knowledge gap" examples expanded

Wilfred lists `HTML Canvas`, `WGSL shaders`, `SVG filters`, `Lottie animations`. The principle these examples expose, named:

> Every domain has its own secret menu — techniques known to insiders but invisible to outsiders.

LLMs execute on what you ask but will not surface the secret menu unprompted. They are reactive, not initiating. They satisfy the prompt; they do not re-write the prompt to be more ambitious than the user knew to ask for.

More examples of secret menus by domain (worth pulling for breadth):

- **Web design** — HTML Canvas, WGSL, SVG filters, Lottie, Houdini paint worklets, view transitions, CSS Custom Highlight API.
- **Audio production** — sidechain compression, mid-side processing, parallel compression, transient design, dynamic EQ.
- **Photography** — focus stacking, exposure bracketing, Brenizer method, dragging the shutter, hyperfocal distance.
- **Cooking** — sous vide, lamination, brining, dry-aging, fermentation, deglazing, fat-washing.
- **Statistics** — bootstrap resampling, Bayesian credible intervals, mixed-effects models, propensity-score matching, multilevel regression.
- **Programming** — region-based memory, structural editing, exhaustiveness checking, property-based testing, time-travel debugging.

A user who knows even one of these terms unlocks a different output from the LLM than a user who does not. The breadth-vocabulary advantage is not a soft-handwave claim; it is a per-keyword empirical fact.

### I. The diversified-portfolio metaphor expanded

Wilfred closes with a portfolio metaphor. The inverse argument worth naming explicitly:

Skill specialisation was always a bet — a bet that the substrate (the tools, the techniques, the dominant problems of the field) would not change so radically that the specialised skill loses value. For most of history this bet paid out. Substrates moved slowly enough that a specialist's investment compounded over decades.

The substrate is now moving in years, not decades. Whole skill categories — copy editing, basic translation, simple coding, certain forms of legal review, formulaic illustration — have been substantially replaced by LLM tooling in 24 months. The specialist who bet entirely on one of those columns has lost the bet. The generalist who held a diversified portfolio has lost a piece of the portfolio but kept the rest.

Risk-adjusted return now favours breadth. This is not a value judgment about specialists — they are still extraordinary at their depth — but it is a structural observation about which strategies survive substrate shifts. In a stable substrate, depth wins. In a churning substrate, breadth wins. We are clearly in the second regime.

### J. The `_`-as-character self-reference

The whole post is named for the underscore character. Worth pointing out that this is not coincidence:

`_` is doing double work in this blog series. In post #4 it is the OpenCues *blank* — the user-placed slot the system fills with whatever the surrounding context demands. In this post it is the *generalist's shape* — flat, neutral, broad, contextually defined by what they are next to.

Both meanings share the same property: a flat slot whose content is determined by context. The OpenCues `_` in `volume _` resolves to a number. The OpenCues `_` in `capital of france _` resolves to a place name. The same character, dispatched by neighbours.

The `_`-shaped person is the same shape applied to humans. Their value is determined by what they are working on. Plug them into a legal context and they prompt for legal vocabulary. Plug them into a music context and they prompt for music vocabulary. The context is what gives the breadth depth-of-the-moment.

Naming the parallel makes the post's title feel intentionally chosen rather than incidentally clever.

### K. "Chosen ones" → polymaths

Wilfred's phrase "only the 'chosen ones' could context switch whilst keeping their skills sharp" is grasping for a term that already exists.

The historical name is *polymath*. Da Vinci, Goethe, Bertrand Russell, Hildegard of Bingen, Ibn Sina, Imhotep. People whose depth across multiple fields was extraordinary by the standard of any single one of those fields, and who somehow produced original work in all of them.

Polymaths have always been rare because the practice cost was prohibitively high. Each domain demanded years of compounding effort to maintain mastery, and the human attention budget per lifetime is finite.

LLMs lower the *maintenance* cost of mastery dramatically. Once a domain has been studied deeply enough to produce a working harness, the harness keeps the practitioner sharp without daily practice. The domain stays accessible.

The post's argument follows: the LLM era democratises polymathy. The "chosen ones" framing softens because the structural barrier (maintenance cost) has dropped. The breadth that was previously available only to people with extraordinary memory and discipline is now available to anyone willing to study the basics of multiple fields and codify the rest into harnesses.
